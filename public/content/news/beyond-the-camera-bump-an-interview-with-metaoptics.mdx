To prove the efficiency that metasurfaces can bring, the company has built a fully functional Android smartphone with an integrated touchless, metalens-based fingerprint sensor around its color metalens modules.

  <div className="mdx-question">
    Anas Chalak (AC): Please briefly introduce yourself and your role at MetaOptics?
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    <b>Mark Thng (MT):</b> I am Mark Thng, Executive Chairman of MetaOptics Technologies. We are headquartered in Singapore and have been listed on the Singapore Exchange (SGX Catalist) since September 2025, with a US entity, MetaOptics Inc., in San Francisco. My role covers corporate strategy, capital allocation, and the partnerships that turn metaoptics research into manufacturable products.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    My career has been spent developing scalable semiconductor and optics solutions for world-class customers, on the manufacturing and product side of this industry rather than in the laboratory. That is also how I see our platform: metaoptics is a wafer-scale semiconductor optics technology, so the same fabrication base serves imaging and sensing. My career at Seagate and Benchmark equipment taught me what it takes to manage a process in volume. My optics years were at Heptagon, culminating in the development of a face authentication module for a flagship smartphone. This was the first time miniaturized optics for 3D sensing had to be qualified at consumer scale, and it taught me that the optical idea is never the hard part. The hard part is yield, repeatability, and supply chain. So, I framed our task simply. Metalenses have been scientifically credible for years. What has been missing is a repeatable high-quality industrial path, and reference hardware that lets customers judge the result with their own eyes. That is what we have been assembling.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-question">
    AC: MetaOptics has built a complete smartphone, not just a component, around your metalens technology. Why build an entire device rather than simply supplying modules to OEMs?
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    <b>MT:</b> The MetaOptics phone is a fully functional 5G smartphone that measures 116mm by 54mm by 6.5mm. The front camera and the fingerprint sensor are 3mm ultra-thin metalens modules, not refractive stacks. It has no camera bump, because we do not believe a phone should have one.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    We introduce metalens technology to the market through our own product demonstrations. A component on a bench invites doubt. A working phone that takes pictures, unlocks with a finger, and sits flat in a pocket settles it. Building the whole device also forces us to own the whole chain, from the nanostructure and the 12-inch glass wafer to the module, the AI pipeline, and the application layer.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    The next step is integrating a pico-projector module directly into the metaoptics phone, combining the two for a truly differentiated device. Remove the lens stack and a phone does not just get thinner – it can do more.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    We are not a phone maker. Metalenses remain our core business. The products are how we prove them.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-question">
    AC: One of the standout features is that the phone has no camera bump. Can you explain, in practical terms, how replacing conventional refractive optics with a metalens eliminates this design constraint that has defined smartphone cameras for over a decade?
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    <b>MT:</b> A conventional camera needs depth. Correcting aberrations takes several curved polymer plastic elements stacked along the optical axis, and that stack is taller than the phone body allows. The bump, the raised island, the reinforcement, all follow from that one requirement.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    A metalens removes the requirement at its source. The focusing element is a few hundred nanometers thick di-electric materials such as silicon nitride or titanium dioxide on glass, so it adds almost nothing to module height. Two design choices compound the effect. Our Gen 2 metalenses are rectangular, matching the sensor exactly instead of being oversized and round. And our RGB architecture puts three metalenses and narrowband filters on one monochrome sensor, so each color channel is acquired independently through its own optical path, without a Bayer mosaic and without demosaicing.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    The result is a flat back and a thinner phone.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-question">
    AC: The phone's 3D non-contact fingerprint sensor also uses your metalens module but relies on just the green channel of the RGB design. Can you walk us through how that works, and what its advantages are?
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    <b>MT:</b> The sensor is the same RGB metalens module, operated as a single-channel imager. Fingerprint ridges are a geometric feature, not a color feature, so we only need one spectral band, and the green channel sits in the most efficient part of the sensor response and the metalens design. The user holds a finger a few centimeters in front of the module, and the pipeline captures the frame, processes it, and matches it against the stored template in real time.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    An added free benefit of the design is anti-spoofing. The three metalenses are laterally displaced on the same substrate, so each one views the fingertip from a slightly different position. Processing provides a 3D view and rejects any printed images or flat replicas. Conventional systems need a time-of-flight sensor or a dot projector to obtain that depth cue.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    The case for touchless is straightforward. There is no platen to degrade, and it provides better response for dry, dirty, or damaged fingers. The same principle extends to face authentication.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-question">
    AC: The current demo units feature a metalens front camera on a 2-megapixel sensor, but you've said the first consumer version will be 5-megapixels, with a 12-megapixel version already in development. What is driving that roadmap, and what changes at each resolution step for a metalens system specifically?
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    <b>MT:</b> The roadmap is driven by customers. 2 MP is enough to prove the architecture and to run the biometric and gesture applications. 5 MP is the level at which a front camera is credible for video calling. 12 MP is where customers have been pushing us.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    What changes at each step is the interaction between the optic and the pixel. As resolution rises on a given sensor format, pixel pitch shrinks, so the metalens point spread function has to be tighter to keep the modulation transfer function above the sampling limit. That drives refinements in fabrication and on the software side.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    The enabling factor behind all of it is fabrication on 12-inch glass substrates using standard deep-UV lithography. Higher resolution only becomes a commercial conversation once that manufacturing base exists.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-question">
    AC: You mentioned evaluation units have already shipped to customers in Europe and Japan. What feedback have you received so far, and what still needs to happen before this becomes a commercial product?
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    <b>MT:</b> Evaluation units have shipped to customers in Europe and Japan, and we are now collecting their feedback. We created the product rather than relying on specifications because the questions that decide design wins are the ones that can only be answered through use: how the camera behaves in different lighting, and how the processing pipeline drops into their software stack.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    The recurring themes so far are the ones we expected. Thickness and the absence of a bump were accepted, and interest has moved quickly to higher-resolution 12 MP modules. And customers want to know exactly where the AI processing runs.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    However, before this becomes a commercial product, three things have to happen. The feedback from these evaluation units has to be folded into the module specification. The higher-resolution modules have to be qualified rather than demonstrated. And the processing pipeline has to be delivered in the form each customer's platform requires, whether as a compiled library or as an SDK.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-question">
    AC: Beyond the optics themselves, you've mentioned using AI-based processing to refine image quality from the metalens. What role does that processing play, and is it something that runs on-device or as part of the camera module?
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    <b>MT:</b> The processing is part of the optical design. A single-layer flat optic loses the aberration correction that a multi-element stack buys with glass, so image quality is restored computationally.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    Three pieces do the work. Classical processing fuses the three sub-aperture images into a single color frame. A deep restoration model then recovers sharpness and uniform color rendition in a single inference pass. In addition, we add two functions: a spatial attention scheduler that concentrates capacity on the outer field, where most restoration models underperform, and a reinforcement-learning illumination adapter that adjusts the input illumination profile at inference time.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    The results are measurable rather than subjective. On a resolution chart, moving from basic to AI-enhanced processing roughly doubles dynamic range, halves edge width, and reduces the noise level by roughly a factor of four. Benchmarked against a current 50MP smartphone, the smartphone has higher sensor resolution and its optical stack produces sharper images, while contrast, color quality, and other image quality metrics are comparable.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    Customers have a choice on where the AI runs. It can sit in the camera module, on the application processor, or in their own application via an SDK. Longer term, most of the acceleration available on a modern phone is still untapped by metalens cameras, so there is considerable headroom left on both quality and power.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-question">
    AC: Is there anything else you'd like to share with our readers?
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    <b>MT:</b> Two points. The first is that metaoptics technology is now shipping in high-volume consumer devices. Our contribution is to push metaoptics from the illumination and 3D sensing path into the imaging path, which is a harder problem because it demands to cover or sample the visible spectral range and to provide photographic image quality.
  </div>

  <div className="mdx-answer">
    The second is that this is now a manufacturing and qualification story rather than a physics story. Fabrication on 12-inch glass substrates with standard deep-UV lithography gives metalenses a genuine path to mass production for applications like smartphone cameras, and that is what makes the roadmap credible.
  </div>
